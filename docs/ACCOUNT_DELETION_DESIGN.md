# Account deletion — fence and drain

Drafted 8 October 2026 against main `06e6f6a3` for #469 (decision 12 in [DURABLE_REQUEST_DESIGN.md](DURABLE_REQUEST_DESIGN.md) §12). **Status: design APPROVED by the product owner on 8 October 2026 (decisions in §9). Not implemented.** No endpoint, migration or job described here exists yet. As with the durable-request design, the exact DDL, endpoint payloads and UI copy are reviewed in the implementation PRs, and implementation, migrations and API changes each need owner approval.

Read [DAEMON.md](DAEMON.md) for identity and [GLOSSARY.md](GLOSSARY.md) for terms. This design settles account deletion's retention, backup and cascading-deletion questions (listed as *Remaining decisions* in [DAEMON_VISION_DECISIONS.md](DAEMON_VISION_DECISIONS.md)); retention for other purposes remains open there.

## 1. Problem

Every user-owned table references `users(id) ON DELETE CASCADE` (conversations, messages, memories, entities, web snapshots, entitlement accounts and ledger rows, auth devices, durable tasks and their attempts, operations and events). Deleting the `users` row is therefore one statement, and it is unsafe while work is in flight:

- A durable attempt can be inside a provider call or a **material tool call** (a notification, a reminder, a document) when the cascade removes its task row. The task's operation fence and its evidence vanish, yet the worker can still complete the external effect before its next fenced write notices the row is gone.
- Background jobs (memory extraction, dreaming, skill evaluation, titles, summaries, entity resolution, home suggestions) can be queued or running for the account and would write to, or fail on, rows that are gone.
- Open compute reservations would be deleted unsettled, losing the conservative charge record.
- Some account data does not cascade at all: every hosted account owns a personal tenant whose `owner_user_id` is `ON DELETE RESTRICT` (so deleting the user row fails), the identity audit log keeps `normalized_email` and `provider_subject` with `user_id` set to `NULL`, email challenges and signup invites are keyed by email, learned skills (`skill_projections` and the files in `data/skills`) carry no owner, and reminders are kept in one shared file (`data/reminders.json`) with no account reference at all.
- Data outside PostgreSQL (generated files on disk, queued job payloads in Redis, backups, the browser, grants held at third parties, logs kept by external tool services) is not reached by the cascade at all.

Conversation deletion was settled separately (decision 11, implemented in #466). This document covers deleting the whole account, and resetting it (§10).

## 2. Principles

1. **Fence before purge.** Once deletion is requested, no new work starts and no running work can publish or start another effect, before anything is removed. The fence covers every account-scoped write, not only durable tasks (§4.1). A database fence cannot recall an external call already admitted or in flight (a notification already handed to its service): such an effect may still complete, and it is what the uncertain-effects report is for (§5).
2. **Reuse the suspension fence.** Slice 1 already makes account suspension stop every durable path: the heartbeat reports it and the attempt stops, the effect fence refuses material operations, `begin_execution` and preparation refuse, and publication ends `failed`/`account_suspended`. Deletion builds on that instead of adding a parallel mechanism.
3. **Uncertain effects are disclosed, not silently erased.** An operation that may have happened is reported to the owner before the evidence is purged (§5).
4. **Deletion means everything learned from the account, and we say what is kept.** Derived data (memories, entities, summaries, embeddings, learned skills) goes with its sources; nothing learned from the account survives elsewhere, since Daemon does not train models on account data. The deletion screen lists exactly what is retained and why (§7).
5. **Resumable and idempotent.** Deletion is a durable job with persisted phases; a crash or restart resumes it, and repeating a phase is harmless.
6. **Bounded.** Every wait has a deadline derived from existing bounds (lease, attempt timeout, reservation recovery), after which the protocol proceeds safely because the database fence already holds.

## 3. State machine

A new `account_deletions` row (one per deletion attempt, not cascaded from `users`) records the request and its phase. At most one attempt per account is open (not `completed` or `restored`), enforced by a partial unique index; a restored attempt stays closed, and a later request starts a new row. Phases advance forward, except that a deletion in its grace period can be cancelled by a restore (§4.4):

| Phase | Entered when | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `requested` | The owner confirms deletion (fresh re-authentication, export offered first) | Row written, recording the account's prior status and suspension reason; the account is suspended for deletion; other sessions revoked; third-party grants revoked (bounded, §4.1); Daemon-managed billing cancelled (bounded, §4.1); `requested` is appended to the journal outside the database (§6.6). |
| `fenced` | Suspension committed | New submissions are refused; queued tasks are cancelled; queued background jobs for the account become no-ops (§4.2). |
| `drained` | No task holds an unexpired lease, no account-scoped background job is running and no reservation is open, or the drain deadline passed | Running attempts and jobs have stopped (or their leases lapsed); reservations are settled or recovered (§4.3). |
| `reported` | The uncertain-effects report is produced | Operations whose outcome is `started`/`unknown` are summarised for the owner (§5). |
| `grace` | Immediately after `reported`; `grace_started` with its deadline is appended to the journal | Seven days. The account stays suspended with its data intact; the owner can sign in to download the report and an export, or restore the account (§4.4). A legal or abuse deletion (decision 6) has a grace period of zero: it records its reason, sends the report (§9 decision 6), offers no restore, and proceeds to `purged`; its journal entry carries `no_grace` so replay never offers a restore either. |
| `purged` | The grace period ended (an unconfirmed billing cancellation does not hold it, §4.1) | The `users` row is deleted (cascade); out-of-database data is removed (§6); `purged` is appended to the journal. |
| `completed` | Propagation finished | Only the non-content deletion record remains (§7). |
| `restored` | The owner restored during `grace` | The deletion's suspension cause is removed; any other cause (from before the request or imposed during grace) stays (§4.1); the deletion record is closed and `restored` is appended to the journal. Cancelled tasks stay cancelled. |

The deletion job runs on the existing arq worker, woken like durable tasks (a sweep finds deletions not yet `completed` or `restored`, including grace periods that have ended), and holds a lease on the `account_deletions` row so only one worker advances it. Each phase is a separate transaction.

## 4. Fence, drain and grace

### 4.1 Fence
- Before confirming, the owner is offered the product's **full context export**, everything [DAEMON.md](DAEMON.md) promises is portable: conversations and messages, memories and entities, files and generated artifacts with their metadata, projects, durable tasks and their results, preferences and settings, learned skills, and reminders. The deletion and reset implementations depend on that export; until it exists for a class of context, that class is named on the confirmation screen as not exportable. The owner is told exactly what deletion removes and what is retained (§7).
- In one transaction: insert the deletion row, add a **deletion suspension** to the entitlement account (`entitlement_accounts.status = 'suspended'`), and revoke auth devices and sessions other than the confirming one.
- In the same phase, outside the transaction: **revoke every third-party grant Daemon holds for the account** (OAuth tokens such as the Google sign-in grant, and any connected-app authorisations as they are added) at the provider's revocation endpoint. Revocation is retried with backoff for at most 24 hours. A grant still unconfirmed after that (an outage or a permanent error) is listed in the deletion report with instructions for revoking it at the provider. Either way, stored credentials are deleted no later than purge, so a failing provider can never strand a deletion or keep a credential alive.
- **Cancel Daemon-managed billing** so nothing renews. Cancellation is idempotent (keyed by the deletion id) and bounded like revocation: retried with backoff for at most 24 hours. An outcome still unconfirmed after that (a timeout or a permanent rejection) is recorded as `billing_unconfirmed`, listed in the deletion report with instructions for cancelling at the processor, and raised to the operator. Purge is not held back by it: it runs when grace ends as promised. Until cancellation is confirmed, the deletion record keeps only the processor's subscription reference (no personal data), so the operator can still cancel it after purge, and any charge taken after the request is refunded. The reference is kept until the processor confirms the subscription is terminated or that none exists; removing it earlier would leave Daemon unable to cancel a later renewal or refund it. If that is still unconfirmed 90 days after the request, the reference moves to a **billing-remediation record** owned by the operator (the processor reference only, no account identity), kept until termination is confirmed and then deleted; the deletion screen discloses this. It is listed among the retained data (§7). A subscription billed through an app store that Daemon cannot cancel is named on the confirmation screen with instructions, rather than left to surprise the owner.
- From this commit no new work for the account reaches a provider: request-bound work is refused by compute admission, and durable acceptance refuses the account outright, so nothing new is queued. Replays still resolve, since a retry of an earlier accepted request must not run anew, and observers keep working so the owner can watch work stop.
- **Suspensions are kept per cause.** Deletion and reset add their own suspension cause and remove only that cause; they never overwrite or clear another. A billing, abuse or operator suspension recorded before the request, or imposed during grace, therefore survives a restore or a reset. The account is active only when no cause remains. (The current schema holds one status and reason; the implementation adds per-cause suspension records, a schema change needing approval.) The prior status and reason are still recorded on the deletion row and in the journal (§6.6), for restoring a backup that predates the request.
- **Every account-scoped write is fenced, not only tasks.** A shared guard checks the account's deletion and suspension state inside the transaction of every account-scoped commit (conversation and message writes, memory and entity writes, summaries, titles, skills, snapshots, files), and every account-scoped write route refuses a suspended account. A background job that started before the fence therefore cannot publish after it, and no route can add content while a deletion or reset purges it.
- **The one exception is the restricted sign-in.** A fresh sign-in must write a device and a session, and a suspended account would otherwise be locked out of its own restore. While a deletion is in `grace` (or a reset awaits acknowledgement), the identity service may create exactly one kind of session for the account: a restricted session that can only read the deletion status, report and export, and restore (or acknowledge and cancel a reset). Writing it is the identity path's only permitted write; profile updates at sign-in (such as a changed provider email) are deferred until the account is active again, and every other route refuses the restricted session.

### 4.2 Cancel
- Queued durable tasks are cancelled in the same way as conversation deletion cancels them (terminal `cancelled`, no attempt starts).
- Running tasks receive a cancel request; their attempts also see the suspension at the next heartbeat (at most 10 s) and stop, after the 2 s stop grace if a provider is mid-call.
- Background jobs: each user-scoped job checks the account's deletion state at start and exits without effect when a deletion exists, and its final writes pass the shared guard above. Checking only the current state is not enough once a reset ends and the account is active again, and an expired heartbeat does not prove a process has stopped. So each user-scoped job (and each durable attempt, which already has its lease epoch) captures the account's **content generation** when it starts, and every publication and file write checks it in the same transaction. Deletion and reset increment the generation at the fence, so a writer that started earlier is refused at its next write, however long it was paused. A filesystem write cannot share a database transaction, so files are fenced by **namespace**: each content generation of an account has its own artifact namespace (a randomly named directory, mapped to the account and generation in the database), a writer stages each file and publishes it by an atomic rename into the namespace it captured, and no writer can create a namespace. Elapsed job lifetime cannot prove a suspended process will never resume, and a database check followed by unrelated filesystem operations is not a fence, so files are fenced by the storage protocol itself, implemented in **one artifact store** that every writer must use:
  - **Namespaces are random and writers never create them.** Only the store creates an account's namespace, when a content generation starts, under a random name recorded in the database; a writer that finds its namespace missing fails.
  - **The store owns every handle.** Staging files are opened only through the store, which tracks open handles per namespace. Removing a path does not reclaim an unlinked file a process still holds open, so a deletion completes only when the namespace has no open handle: a handle not released within a deadline gets its owning process terminated, and on Linux the store also scans process file descriptors for the namespace and quarantine paths before declaring completion.
  - **Staging is inside the namespace** (its own staging area), never elsewhere, and publication is a rename within that namespace.
  - **The writable namespace is retired at the fence, not at purge.** A database check followed by filesystem work is not atomic, so when the fence commits the store atomically renames the account's live namespace to a new random **frozen** name (recorded in the database): writers that captured the old name find nothing to stage or publish into, while the frozen contents stay readable for the export and for a restore. Staging files are discarded from the frozen namespace (only published files are kept). A restore or cancelled reset makes the frozen contents the new generation's namespace under another new random name; a purge deletes them.
  - **Removal.** Purge (and reset) atomically rename the frozen namespace, staging area included, to a quarantine name, then delete the quarantine completely once no handle into it remains; a removed directory accepts no new entries. Random names (128 bits) are never reused, so an absent name is a retired one: no marker file is left.
  - **Every namespace transition is write-ahead.** A rename and a database commit cannot be atomic together, so before each rename (live → frozen at the fence, frozen → quarantine at purge, frozen → a new live namespace on restore or cancellation) the store durably records a **storage-transition record** on the non-cascading deletion row: the operation and generation, the source name, the preallocated random destination name and a status (`intended`, then `renamed`, then `committed`). Only then does it rename, then mark the record `renamed`, then commit the new mapping and mark it `committed`. After a crash, reconciliation is idempotent: it checks which of source and destination exists and finishes or redoes the transition, so the destination is never lost even if the mapping commit failed. The account stays fenced until every transition record is `committed`, and the cleanup manifest (§6.3) includes every destination named in these records.
  - **Verification is scoped to the operation**: the job checks only the namespaces and quarantines this deletion or reset recorded in its cleanup manifest (§6.3), never the whole store.
  - This protocol relies on atomic directory rename on one POSIX filesystem. Object storage, or several hosts, needs an equivalent approved primitive (conditional writes keyed by generation, or a single fenced storage service); providing it is part of the storage-contract prerequisite in §6.2.
- Running user-scoped jobs record a heartbeat against the account, so the drain can wait for them (§4.3). (Implementation note: one shared guard and one job registry used by every user-scoped job, tested per job.)

### 4.3 Drain
- Wait until no task of the account is `running` with an unexpired lease, no user-scoped background job of the account has a live heartbeat, and no `entitlement_reservations` row of the account is `open`.
- **Deadline:** lease (45 s) + attempt timeout (600 s, which also exceeds the background job timeout) + reservation recovery (2 × `request_timeout_s`), about 13 minutes with current settings. After it, any remaining lease has expired, so the database fence already rejects every write from a stale worker; remaining open reservations are settled at their full hold (the conservative rule already used for lost attempts).
- The owner sees "Stopping your running work" with the count of tasks still stopping.

### 4.4 Grace and restore
- The grace period is **seven days** from `reported`. During it the account is suspended: no work runs and no new work is accepted, but its data is intact.
- **Restore:** signing in during grace (a fresh sign-in, not an existing session) shows only the deletion status, the report, the export and *Restore account*. Confirming restore removes the deletion's suspension cause (any other cause, including one imposed during grace, stays), closes the deletion record as `restored` and appends `restored` to the journal (§6.6). Revoked third-party grants and cancelled billing are not reinstated automatically; the owner reconnects and resubscribes. Cancelled tasks stay cancelled.
- When grace ends, the job proceeds to purge. There is no restore after that.
- **Restore and purge cannot both happen.** Both contend on the same row lock with one conditional transition: a restore commits only if the phase is still `grace` and the grace deadline has not passed; the worker advances to purge only if the phase is still `grace` and the deadline has passed. Exactly one commits; the other sees the changed phase and stops (a late restore is told the account was deleted). Each transition appends its journal entry while holding that lock, before committing (§6.6). A row lock alone is not enough across a failed commit (a restore appended just before the deadline whose commit failed would otherwise lose to a purge that takes the lock before the sweep reconciles it), so every transition, under the lock and **before evaluating its condition, first applies any journal entry of its operation that the database does not record as applied**. The journal also refuses a conflicting entry: each append names the operation's previous entry and succeeds only if that is still the operation's latest (a compare-and-append), so two terminal entries for one operation can never both be recorded. Exactly one terminal path wins, in the journal and in the database.

## 5. Uncertain effects

Before grace, the job collects operations whose outcome is `started` or `unknown` (an effect that may have happened), plus `needs_attention` tasks. Material tool calls made by request-bound (foreground) chat must produce the same evidence: today the operation fence is installed only for durable tasks, so the implementation installs it on foreground material calls too (an operation row per call, keyed by the request), and the drain waits for in-flight foreground requests through their open reservations. Until then, the report would miss a foreground effect, so this is a prerequisite of the deletion implementation. A material tool that returns an error is recorded `unknown`, not `succeeded`, unless it states that it did not perform the action (the effect fence's `_outcome_of`, on main since #478): a notification whose response was lost can still have been delivered, so it is reported. Each item lists the tool name, a target summary and timestamps — never message content. The summary stored at the fence today copies up to 200 characters of a URL, topic, title or name, which can carry prompt text, identifiers or secrets, so the report does not reuse it as is: each material tool declares the fields that are safe to report (such as a notification channel's kind, or a URL's host without path or query), and existing operation rows are reduced to those fields (anything else redacted) before they appear in the report.

The report is shown on the deletion screen and stays downloadable throughout the grace period. **It is discarded with the account at purge; no tombstones are kept** (decision 2).

## 6. Purge and propagation

1. **Database, in order, in one transaction:**
   1. financial records the law requires to keep are copied out anonymised (decision 3);
   2. records that do not cascade are removed: the account's identity-audit rows, email challenges, Google nonce challenges and signup invites. The login email can change (a verified Google email change updates `users.normalized_email`), and a released address can later belong to another account, so the job first reconstructs **each address the account has had and the interval it held it**: the current one, and those in its identity-audit and provider-identity history. The implementation records each email change in the identity audit (old and new address, time) so the intervals are complete. Identity-audit rows are removed by user id and provider subject, and by address only within the account's interval for it; challenges and invites likewise only where created within that interval. The challenge that created the account predates its user row, and a Google sign-in nonce is consumed without recording the user, so the implementation records the claimed user id on each consumed email challenge and Google nonce, and purge deletes those rows by it (older consumed rows without it fall to the finite sweep below). Invites linked to the account directly (`signup_invites.used_by_user_id` or `created_by_user_id`, which the user deletion would otherwise set to null) are deleted first, including an invite the account created for someone else's address, so no attributable invite or still-active signup credential is left. Rows created while an address belonged to another account are never touched. For accounts whose email changed before that audit existed, the overwritten address cannot be recovered, so its rows cannot be attributed safely: they are not deleted by the account's purge but are bounded by their own retention instead. That retention does not exist yet: no sweep deletes `email_challenges` or `signup_invites` (invites are only marked `expired`), and the identity audit's retention is an open decision in [DAEMON_VISION_DECISIONS.md](DAEMON_VISION_DECISIONS.md). So a finite purge of expired challenges and expired or consumed invites, and an owner decision on identity-audit retention, are prerequisites of the deletion implementation; until both exist, the deletion screen states that such records under an earlier email may be kept indefinitely;
   3. the account's worker failure audits (`job_failures`): the table has no user reference, while job ids, arguments and error fields can carry account identifiers and failure details. The implementation records the owning user on each failure of a user-scoped job, and records a multi-user job's failure (such as scheduled dreaming, which reports one aggregate result) as one row per affected user, so the purge deletes exactly the account's rows. An older aggregate row is not deleted: the purge redacts the account's identifiers and error entries from it, keeping other users' evidence; older rows without it are matched by the user, conversation and task ids collected before the purge (the same set used for Redis, step 3 below);
   4. account-owned learned skills: their `skill_projections` rows and canonical files in `data/skills` (skills shipped with Daemon or shared by an operator are not account-owned and are kept). This needs an owner/provenance column on skill projections, added by the implementation. Learning from an account must never change a skill it does not own: today autonomous refinement can patch a manual, imported or system skill from a user's turn, so the implementation makes such refinement produce an account-owned copy instead, leaving the shared skill untouched. That copy needs an owner-qualified identity: today `skill_projections.skill_id` is a global key, canonical files use a global slug path, and candidate selection and listing read skills without a user filter. So the implementation scopes account-owned skill keys and files by owner, and filters every skill read, mutation and consolidation path by owner while still offering explicitly shared skills; two accounts refining the same skill get separate copies, and one account's copy is never selected for another. Edits already made that way cannot be attributed to an account; they are disclosed as possibly retained;
   5. the account's devices and sessions (they reference the personal tenant with `ON DELETE RESTRICT`, and the confirming session is kept until purge), then the personal tenant's memberships, tenant-owned rows and the personal tenant itself, which otherwise blocks the user deletion (`ON DELETE RESTRICT`);
   6. the `users` row, whose cascade removes every remaining user-owned row: conversations and messages, memories and their embeddings, entities and the relationship graph, summaries, dream, extraction and retrieval logs, web snapshots, home suggestions, durable tasks with their attempts, operations and events, entitlement rows and auth devices.
2. **Generated files** (documents, images, audio, video): removed by owner namespace in the same job, then verified by listing **every storage location that can hold them**. A listing on the deletion worker's own disk proves nothing about other hosts, and the storage choice for artifacts is still pending in the durable-request design (shared storage or object storage, design choice C in §13 of [DURABLE_REQUEST_DESIGN.md](DURABLE_REQUEST_DESIGN.md)); so an approved storage contract (a verified shared filesystem, object storage, or a registry of where each artifact's copies live) is a prerequisite of the deletion implementation, and `completed` is reached only when every location is verified empty. Verification and provider deletion requests are retried with backoff for at most 24 hours after grace ends. A location or provider still unconfirmed then is raised to the operator, recorded on the deletion row (location and asset ids only) and completed manually; the deletion screen says in advance that such a rare case is finished by the operator rather than by the stated deadline. **Media hosted by providers** is separate: image and video generation through xAI and fal.ai can return provider-hosted URLs rather than local files, so the implementation records each provider asset or job id with its owner, requests deletion where the provider supports it, and otherwise discloses that provider's output retention on the deletion screen. The existing `cleanup_generated_files`/`cleanup_generated_images` cron jobs remain the backstop. **Legacy root-level files** that predate owner prefixes have no trustworthy owner, and the accepted legacy-artifact contract ([DURABLE_REQUEST_DESIGN.md](DURABLE_REQUEST_DESIGN.md)) forbids assigning or deleting them by inference. Until that contract's reconciliation decides their owners, account deletion cannot remove them; they stay inaccessible (unowned files are denied) and the deletion screen lists them as possibly retained account data. **Reminders** are stored today in one shared file with no account reference, so neither the cascade nor an owner prefix can find them: the implementation first moves reminder storage to account-owned rows (or adds an owner to each record), and both purge and reset then remove the account's reminders.
3. **Redis:** live task channels need no action (they carry no data at rest); per-user rate-limit, budget and cache keys are deleted by pattern. The shared web-fetch cache (`fetch:v2` keys, holding a requested URL and its extracted content) has no owner, so a pattern cannot find the account's entries: the implementation records, per account, the fetch-cache keys its requests wrote, and purge and reset evict them (another account's later hit on the same URL simply refetches). Until then, such entries expire with the cache's TTL, which the deletion screen discloses. Queued, deferred and finished arq job entries for the account are **deleted, not just made no-ops**, because some carry content in their arguments (`generate_title` enqueues the user's message): before the database purge the job collects the account's conversation and task identifiers and deletes the matching job and result keys. Everything outside the database that the purge must reach is first written as a **cleanup manifest** on the deletion row, which does not cascade with the user: the Redis key names, the account's artifact namespaces (live, frozen and quarantine names) and its provider-hosted asset and job ids (identifiers only, never content). The database purge commits only after the manifest is durable, so a restarted job can always find what to remove even though the cascade has deleted the database mappings. Redis eviction is completed before the purge commits. Late writes are prevented as well as cleaned: user-scoped jobs run without stored results (no arq result key), and the manifest is kept, with eviction repeated, until every registered user-scoped job of the account is proven terminated (its process exited or was terminated, §4.2), not merely past the drain deadline. The manifest is cleared only after that and after artifact and provider cleanup are verified. The implementation also changes user-scoped jobs to carry identifiers only, never content. Results of multi-user jobs (such as the scheduled dreaming run, whose result is kept an hour under a shared job id) carry counts only, never user ids or error text; per-user details go to the per-user failure rows (§6.1 step 3). Until that change, such a result expires within its one-hour result lifetime and is disclosed as such.
4. **Browser:** the confirming session signs out, which clears drafts and pending-submission keys (and held submissions, with #479). Other clients cannot be cleaned from the server: their drafts live in a tab's `sessionStorage` and their attachments in IndexedDB, and local cleanup runs only on a local sign-in or sign-out. So each client stores the account's content generation with its drafts, and the next authenticated response that shows a newer generation (or a revoked session, which signs the client out locally) discards them. A client that never reconnects keeps its drafts until its tab closes and its attachments until that browser profile next loads Daemon (expired attachments are swept only on load, so a profile that never loads it again keeps them indefinitely); the deletion screen discloses this client-local data, which Daemon cannot reach, and that it may persist until the owner clears that browser's site data. The service worker never stores backend responses (with #482, for #474) **or protected generated artifacts on any origin**: `/generated-images/`, `/generated-files/` and `/generated-audio/` are network-only whether they are requested from the backend or through the app's own origin, and entries an earlier service worker cached are removed when it activates. Two browser stores are involved. The service worker's **Cache Storage** is handled by #484 (merged): private responses are never stored there, every request the worker controls is refetched with `no-store`, and earlier entries are purged on activation. The browser's **HTTP cache** is not fully handled yet: since #484 every artifact response is `Cache-Control: private, no-store`, but responses cached before that can still answer requests the worker does not control (the first load before it activates, browsers without a service worker, development builds) until they expire. Closing that path (for example `Clear-Site-Data: "cache"` on sign-out and account change) is tracked in #486 and is a **prerequisite** of the deletion and reset privacy claim, together with a real-browser regression for an upgrade and an account switch with a pre-existing HTTP-cached artifact, covering uncontrolled requests. Only then does no cached account data remain in the browser; this is not established by #484 alone.
5. **Providers:** default inference routes are zero-data-retention (DEC12), so no inference provider holds the account's content, and Daemon does not train models on account data. Provider-side deletion requests for inference are out of scope until DEC12's opt-in retention routes exist (decision 5). **Tool services are different:** the default web search service (Brave) is used under an approved standard-retention exception and keeps API query logs for up to 90 days ([SEARCH_SERVICE_APPROVALS.md](SEARCH_SERVICE_APPROVALS.md)); search queries can contain account content, and Daemon cannot delete them. The deletion screen discloses each retained tool service and its period.
6. **Backups and the deletion journal:** a backup taken before purge still contains the account, and a backup taken before the request does not even contain the `account_deletions` row. Every deletion is therefore also recorded in a **deletion journal kept outside the database and its backups** (append-only, its own durable store), holding only the opaque user id, the deletion id, a keyed hash of each login identifier, the account's complete effective suspension-cause set at the time of each entry, recorded in `requested` and `reset_requested` as well as in terminal entries (so a restore can return to every cause in force even from a backup with no deletion row), and timestamps. Each entry has a sequence number and an operation id (the deletion or reset it belongs to). The journal records each **transition** as well as the request: `requested`, then `grace_started` (with the grace deadline, known only then), then `restored` or `purged`; for a reset (§10), `reset_requested`, then `reset_acknowledged` (the owner acknowledged the report) and `reset_purged`, or `reset_cancelled`. While a deletion or reset is open, every change to the account's suspension causes (a billing, abuse or operator suspension added or lifted) is also journaled (`suspension_added`, `suspension_removed`), and each terminal entry records the account's complete effective cause set, so replay from any backup restores exactly the causes in force.

   **Write order.** The database transition and the journal append cannot share a transaction, so the journal is written first and is authoritative: each transition takes the deletion row's lock, checks its condition, appends its journal entry (durably), then commits, recording that entry's sequence number as **applied** in the same database transaction (a small non-cascading `deletion_journal_applied` table), and only then is the owner told. If the append fails, the transaction rolls back and nothing is acknowledged. If the commit fails after the append (a crash), the deletion sweep finds a journal entry the database does not reflect and re-applies it; every transition is idempotent, and a restore entry carries its own timestamp, which was within the grace deadline when appended. So no outcome is acknowledged before it is durable outside the database, and a journal entry is never left unapplied. **A new request has no deletion row to lock yet**, so requests serialize on the account instead: a deletion or reset request takes an account-scoped lock (the account's entitlement row), first applies any unapplied journal entry of any operation of the account, and only then appends its own `requested` (or `reset_requested`). The journal chains these per account as well as per operation (the append names the account's latest entry), so a retry after a failed commit finds the earlier request already applied and is answered with that deletion, never a second one.

   **Backups are kept for 30 days**, and every restore replays the journal before the restored database serves traffic (decision 4). Replay applies, **in sequence order, every journal entry the restored database does not record as applied**, not only the latest one, and never infers application from timestamps: an entry appended just before a backup but committed after it (or never) is not recorded as applied in that backup, so it is replayed. Each applies its own operation: `requested` fences the account, re-runs the idempotent cancellation phase (every non-terminal task of the account accepted before the request is cancelled, as at the original fence) and starts its deletion with the recorded grace deadline and prior status; `restored` (applied only after the replayed `requested` has re-cancelled the account's earlier tasks, so cancelled tasks stay cancelled) closes it and removes the deletion's suspension cause (in a backup that predates the request, where no cause was added, the recorded prior status is what the backup already holds); `purged` purges; `reset_requested`, `reset_acknowledged`, `reset_purged` and `reset_cancelled` do the same for a reset (an applied acknowledgement means the timeout can no longer cancel it); `suspension_added` and `suspension_removed` update the cause set. Entries the backup records as applied are skipped, so a reset already in the backup never erases content created after it. A cancellation only cancels its own operation: a later restored deletion or cancelled reset never masks an earlier reset or purge the backup predates. A `requested` entry without `grace_started` resumes the deletion from the fence; its grace deadline is computed when it reaches grace again. A reset's purge replays as a content cutoff: content the backup holds from before the reset's fence is removed, and the account itself is kept.

   **Journal retention follows the backups that actually exist, not the request.** The 30-day backup retention is not enforced today: `scripts/backup_db.py` writes timestamped dumps and never deletes them. So an enforced, verified backup-expiry contract (rotation that deletes every dump past 30 days, plus an inventory of the oldest dump still held) is a prerequisite of the deletion implementation. Retention is decided by **applied-state evidence, never by comparing times**: a backup taken after a journal append but before its commit postdates the entry yet lacks its effect. So the backup inventory records, for each retained dump, the set of `deletion_journal_applied` markers that dump contains (read from the dump's own consistent snapshot). An account's entries may be removed only when it has a terminal entry (`purged`, `restored`, `reset_purged` or `reset_cancelled`) and **every retained backup records all of that account's entries as applied**; a dump that lacks a marker keeps the entries until rotation removes it. If the inventory or a dump's marker set is unavailable, nothing is removed (fail closed). With 30-day rotation, entries are thus kept until 30 days after the last dump without their markers, at least until day 37 for a purge on day 7.

**What the owner is told:** the account's content leaves Daemon's live systems when the seven-day grace period ends, and leaves backups within 30 days after that. Grace starts once running work has stopped and revocations and billing are settled or reported: at most 24 hours (the retry bound) plus the drain deadline (about 13 minutes with current settings) after the request. So content leaves live systems no later than 8 days plus the drain deadline after the request, and backups no later than 38 days plus the drain deadline; the screen states these bounds as "within 8 days and 1 hour" and "within 38 days and 1 hour", except for a storage location or provider that cannot confirm deletion in time (§6.2), which the operator completes. Separately, search queries already sent to the web search service may remain in its logs for up to 90 days after they were made.

## 7. Record of the deletion

After `completed`, only the `account_deletions` row and its `deletion_journal_applied` markers remain: an opaque deletion id, the phase timestamps and counts (tasks cancelled, operations reported, files removed), and the sequence numbers of the journal entries applied. The markers are kept (in the database, so in every backup taken after them) until their journal entries and every backup that could depend on them have expired, so a backup taken after a reset or purge always shows those entries as applied and replay never repeats them. It holds no email, name, content or device data. It exists so a restore can re-apply the deletion and so support can confirm a deletion happened.

Retained beyond the account, and listed as such on the deletion screen:
- the deletion record and applied markers above, and the journal entries (§6.6);
- provider-hosted media outputs where the provider offers no deletion, under that provider's retention (§6.2);
- while a billing cancellation is unconfirmed, the processor's subscription reference, until the processor confirms termination (after 90 days, in an operator billing-remediation record) (§4.1);
- legacy generated files without a trustworthy owner (§6.2), and identity records under an earlier email that cannot be attributed (§6.1), each under its own retention;
- logs kept by external tool services under their own retention, such as web search queries (§6.5), and by the mail provider, which keeps under its own retention the identity emails sent to the account (sign-in codes, new-device notices with device label, platform, time and provider) and the report of an operator deletion (§9 decision 6); Daemon requests deletion of those messages and logs where the provider's API offers it, and the deletion screen states the provider's retention for each class;
- Daemon's own application and container logs: they are not selected by account, so the implementation makes logging content-safe (no message content, prompts, provider messages or requested URLs; account ids only as keyed hashes) and keeps logs only for the deployment's bounded rotation period, which the deletion screen states. Lines written before that change may hold identifiers or fragments until rotated;
- edits that account learning made to shared skills before the change in §6.1 step 4, which cannot be attributed;
- financial records only where accounting law requires them (decision 3): if billing runs through a payment processor, the processor holds the invoices and Daemon keeps none; if Daemon itself is the record of charges, an anonymised copy (amounts, dates, plan) under a random accounting id that is generated at purge and stored nowhere else (not the deletion id, not in the journal), so no record links it back to the account, for the statutory period.

## 10. Reset

*Reset* removes the account's content and everything learned from it, but keeps the account, its sign-in and its subscription. It uses the same protocol on the content only: fence (the account is suspended for the duration, recording its prior status and reason as deletion does), cancel, drain, report, then purge. Because the `users` row stays, the cascade does not help: the purge explicitly deletes **every user-scoped row except a keep-list**. The `users` row stays, but only its identity fields (id, login email, verification and sign-in fields) keep their values: its content-bearing columns, such as `users.settings` with preferences and custom instructions, are reset to their defaults, and the catalogue test classifies each `users` column as identity or content. The keep-list is that row, the entitlement account with its ledger and billing, prepaid video credits (`video_credit_balances` and `video_credit_transactions`, purchased value that the commercial contract keeps independently, [SUBSCRIPTION_ARCHITECTURE.md](SUBSCRIPTION_ARCHITECTURE.md)), provider identities and the identity audit, and the open reset row. Everything else that references the account goes, including conversations, messages, memories, entities, summaries, skills, web snapshots, files, tasks, home suggestions, and the derived logs that only the user reference ties to it: `memory_extraction_log` and `retrieval_log` (deleting conversations only nulls their conversation id, while snippets, query text and embeddings remain), `dream_log`, `skill_consolidation_log` and `skill_nudge_user_state`, and the account's `job_failures` rows. The implementation derives the list from the schema's user references and a catalogue test fails when a new one is not classified. Third-party grants are kept. **Every client is signed out when the purge completes**: all sessions and devices are revoked, including the confirming one, so each tab and device must sign in again, and signing in (a new auth generation) clears its local drafts, held submissions and attachments; a client that reconnects with drafts from an older content generation discards them on its next authenticated response (§6.4). A client holding a pre-reset draft therefore cannot write it back once the suspension ends. Reset requires a fresh sign-in and a typed confirmation and offers the export first; it has no grace period. The purge waits for the owner to **acknowledge the uncertain-effects report** (shown on the reset screen and downloadable, and shown again at the next sign-in if the owner left). If it is not acknowledged within seven days, the reset is cancelled, removing only the reset's suspension cause, with the content intact. The owner may also cancel a reset from the restricted session (§4.1) while it awaits acknowledgement. Acknowledgement, owner cancellation, the timeout cancellation and the advance to purge contend on the reset row's lock with one conditional, journaled transition each, as restore and purge do (§4.4): an acknowledgement commits only while the reset is awaiting acknowledgement and its deadline has not passed; the timeout cancels only after the deadline with no acknowledgement; the purge starts only from an acknowledged reset. An owner cancellation commits (and appends `reset_cancelled`) only while the reset is awaiting acknowledgement, so once an acknowledgement is applied the reset can no longer be cancelled and the owner is told the purge is under way. Exactly one wins, and a late acknowledgement or cancellation is told the outcome. As for deletion, each of these first applies any unapplied journal entry of the reset under the lock, and the journal's compare-and-append refuses a second terminal entry. After the purge the report is discarded, as for deletion. When the purge completes, the reset's suspension cause is removed, exactly as when a reset is cancelled: reset lifts only its own suspension, so any other cause (billing, abuse or an operator, from before or during the reset) stays. Reset is recorded in the deletion journal (§6.6), so restoring an earlier backup re-applies it.
